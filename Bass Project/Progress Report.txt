Forward-Facing Sonar and Tournament Performance in the Bassmaster Elite Series (2012–2026)
I am an avid hunter and fisherman. The topic I have chosen is one that I am more interested in than one that applies to my research in poultry and livestock housing.  Forward-facing sonar (FFS) is a real-time fish-finding technology that enables anglers to locate and track fish ahead of their boat, and its rapid adoption in professional bass fishing has sparked debate regarding its influence on tournament outcomes and competitive balance. I became aware of the debate surrounding FFS, which has changed how many professional anglers locate and catch fish around a year and a half ago. Since its widespread adoption around 2018, anglers, tournament organizations, and fans have debated (in a rather heated fashion) whether FFS provides a significant competitive advantage and how it affects tournament outcomes. I would like to use the methods from this course to investigate whether measurable changes in tournament performance are associated with FFS availability and whether those effects vary among fisheries.
My dataset contains approximately 13,968 angler-tournament observations from Bassmaster Elite Series events between 2012 and 2026. Each observation represents an individual angler's performance in a tournament. Variables include season year, tournament identifier, event name, location, standardized waterbody, FFS status, finishing place, fish caught, total catch weight, fantasy points, and several derived metrics. The data were compiled from publicly available tournament results from the Bassmaster website (https://www.bassmaster.com), and I have full access to the dataset and supporting documentation. One challenge is that fishery-health metrics such as CPUE are not currently available for most waterbodies, so the analysis will primarily rely on tournament performance data.
My initial idea is to examine changes in catch metrics over time and compare tournaments where FFS was unavailable, unrestricted, restricted, or prohibited. Potential response variables include total catch weight, average fish weight, and the proportion of the tournament bag limit achieved. During preliminary exploratory analysis, I found that tournament structure may be an important factor because some events are shortened by weather, resulting in different tournament bag limits. Therefore, I expect to include tournament format, season, and waterbody as important explanatory variables. Methods I may use include exploratory visualizations, regression models, mixed-effects models, and model comparison techniques to determine whether associations between FFS availability and tournament outcomes remain after accounting for differences among fisheries and tournament formats.

I have revised my central question to: After accounting for tournament structure, calendar time, and differences among fisheries, do tournament-level catch outcomes differ across forward-facing sonar rule environments?

An important clarification is that my Allowed_FFS variable describes the rule in effect for an entire tournament. It does not indicate whether a particular angler owned or used FFS. FFS availability is also closely connected to calendar time because tournaments held before FFS existed necessarily occurred in earlier years. Other changes in electronics, angler strategies, tournament schedules, and fisheries occurred during the same period. This limits my ability to separate FFS from other changes in the sport. However, I am still optimistic that some insight will be gained by analyzing this dataset.

I would like to state for the record that I am not a follower of the pro fishing sport, just an outdoorsman who likes to fish, so I am going into this with a sort of limited domain knowledge pertaining to the tournament itself. In some follow up research, trying to make sense of some patterns that I found during the exploratory analysis, I also found that cumulative fish counts are affected by the tournament’s advancement structure. I found that the tournaments structure and rules state that competitors fish for a daily limit of five bass. The full field (~100 participants) fishes the first two days, after which only the top 50 highest-ranked anglers advance to the next round. Another cut occurs before the final day, down to 10. As a result, an angler eliminated after Day 2 can weigh a maximum of approximately 10 fish, while anglers advancing through later rounds may weigh 15 or 20 fish. Fish counts of {x ≤10}, {10 ≤ x ≤15}, and {15 ≤ x ≤ 20} therefore reflect participants days fished being 2, 3, or 4, respectively.

The tournament will be my primary independent unit, rather than each angler-tournament row. Anglers in the same tournament share the same fishery, weather, rules, schedule, and FFS status, so treating all angler records as independent would overstate the available evidence. I will first summarize individual results into one row per tournament. Possible tournament-level measures include median average fish weight, median cumulative weight, outcome variability, zero-catch rate, and upper-quartile performance.

The primary course method will be resampling-based comparison. I will use tournament-level bootstrap samples to estimate confidence intervals for differences in outcomes across FFS categories. If comparable tournaments exist under multiple FFS rules, I will consider restricted permutation tests within similar waterbodies, time periods, event formats, or other defensible strata. I will not permute FFS labels across fundamentally different years or fisheries if the required exchangeability assumption is not reasonable.

I will use time-series methods to describe changes in tournament-level outcomes over time. For repeatedly visited waterbodies, I may compare each tournament with the previous event held at that fishery as a simple baseline. This can help determine whether later outcomes differ from the recent history of the same waterbody, although it still will not isolate FFS from all other temporal changes.

I will also investigate similarity and clustering by representing repeatedly visited fisheries using features such as typical average fish weight, outcome variability, field size, and tournament-format characteristics. Any clustering result will be evaluated using cluster sizes, silhouette scores, alternative feature choices, and stability checks. I will not interpret fishery groups if the clusters are small or unstable. Finally, anomaly detection may identify unusually productive or difficult tournaments and determine whether a small number of extreme events are driving the apparent FFS comparisons.

This project cannot determine whether an individual angler benefited from using FFS, nor can it establish that FFS caused a change in tournament outcomes. Individual sonar use, daily catch records, complete environmental conditions, and fishery-health measures are unavailable. However, the revised approach can determine whether tournament-level outcomes are systematically different under observed FFS rule environments, how uncertain those differences are, whether they are consistent over time, and whether they recur across sufficiently represented fisheries.

Professors Response:
Thank you, Josh. This addresses the clarification I requested, and your proposal earns the full 5/5. You have a focused question, a clear role for course methods, and appropriate limits on what the comparisons can establish.

Two cautions as you proceed:

Cumulative fish counts do not uniquely identify days fished, because anglers may catch fewer than the daily limit. Use explicit participation records if available.
Aggregating to one row per tournament addresses dependence among anglers within an event, but repeated fisheries and anglers may still create dependence across tournaments. Keep that structure in mind when choosing resampling units and interpreting uncertainty.
